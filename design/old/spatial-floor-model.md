# Abandoned: the spatial floor model

**Superseded 2026-08-23** by the floor-deck model — see
[ticket 18](../issues/18-floor-encounter-decisions.md). Nothing here is current.

For about a week the floor was a **2D plan the characters moved around on**: rooms you entered, an
enemy you could break off from and come back to, places to scavenge, and a shrinking footprint as the
tower narrowed. The rulings below were made under that assumption and have no home now that a floor
is a deck of cards. They are kept because each was tagged, and because the *reasons* behind several
of them are still live even though the mechanisms are gone.

## What was ruled, and where it came from

- **A floor is one continuous encounter on a 2D floor plan**, ended by ascending.
  `[you, tickets 04 and 05]` The characters run around it over many turns — entering rooms,
  fighting, fleeing, doubling back, being chased.
- **Red and Gray move independently within a floor.** `[you, ticket 05]` They could be in different
  places at once, which was the whole of the co-op spatial question.
- **Scavenged items.** `[you, ticket 05]` Something found during a floor went **straight to hand**
  with **Retain**, occupied hand space, and was discarded on ascending. Scavenging was therefore
  never a healing verb, and everything found was use-it-or-lose-it within the floor.
- **Searching a room costs stamina**, making scavenging a push-your-luck decision rather than a free
  pickup. `[you, ticket 05]`
- **The pyramid tower.** `[you, ticket 06]` The tower narrows as it rises, so higher floors are
  physically tighter — fewer rooms worth scavenging, less space to keep away from enemies. This was
  the human's own proposal and the leading answer to ticket 05's second axis of escalation. It was
  recorded as a strong candidate for ticket 19, explicitly not binding, and ticket 19 never ran.
- **The squeeze works through space and supply, not rate.** `[you, ticket 06]` Fewer places to
  scavenge falls out of the geometry for free; no rule was to make a higher floor cost more per
  action.
- **"You cannot simply leave."** `[you, ticket 06]` A requirement binding on ticket 08: breaking off
  from the floor's enemy had to cost something, so that disengaging was never a free way to switch
  the floor's pressure off. Pursuit was the human's own suggested mechanism, with ticket 08 free to
  choose another.
- **Most moves cost stamina in some form** — including movement between rooms, searching, forcing a
  door, sprinting, tripping, falling. `[you, tickets 04 and 06]` Ticket 07 owned the price list.
- **Whether damage to the floor's enemy persists when the party breaks off contact.** `[you]`
  Deliberately left open by ticket 05 and routed jointly to tickets 08 and 19, because it depended on
  how the enemy was physically tracked. It was load-bearing: ticket 04's picture of a floor — dent it,
  flee, scavenge, come back — existed only if damage persisted.
- **Whether the player sees the whole floor on arrival**, or it is revealed as they move.
  `[you, ticket 06 deferred it]` Routed to tickets 18 and 19; the agent recommended full visibility
  from turn one and that recommendation was never adopted.
- **Hazards as a second thing that acts on the player**, alongside the floor's enemy — a tool, not a
  requirement, with how heavily to use them left to balance time. `[you, ticket 06]` What a hazard
  physically *was* never got settled, because it waited on the floor plan.
- **Table footprint.** Ticket 19 was to produce the map's first hard number for physical area and
  component count. It never ran, so the map has no footprint number at all.

## What outlived the model

Three of the concerns above are older than the spatial floor and survive it, restated against the
deck in the tickets that now own them:

- **You cannot simply leave** was a statement about pressure, not about geography. Under the deck
  model it is structural: the deck has to be beaten to advance, and a card you fail against comes
  back.
- **Escalation needs a second axis** beyond bigger enemy numbers. The pyramid was the spatial answer.
  The deck's answer, if it has one, is composition — and that is an open question, not a decision.
- **Most moves cost stamina.** The principle stands; the specific price list was written against
  rooms and doors and has to be rebuilt against whatever a turn contains now.
